# Familiarity with the concept

An essential step to having a reserve of support is building awareness and familiarity with the concept.  The goals are relatively straightforward: to have people understand that the concept exists, what the concept is, where it has come from, to have some limited working knowledge of the concept, to have some tangible connection to it, and to know how to find out more.

I want to distinguish here between what I mean when I say "awareness" and what I mean when I say "familiarity".  By "awareness" I mean some knowledge of the concept, such as having heard of it ("awareness of the concept") and maybe understanding the mechanics and principles of it ("awareness of how the concept works").  But familiarity goes a step or two further: by familiarity I mean some ability to tangibly or concretely imagine the concept as a lived experience, and one that they carry around with them.

For example, if a teacher describes an artistic technique a student may be able to fully understand the technique, and this is what I mean by "awareness".  But in some sense that technique may not become "real" for them until they have the chance to see examples of it, or try it themselves.  At that point they are moving from awareness to familiarity.  But full familiarity is when they can instantly, and perhaps even unconsciously, identify when the technique is used or when to use it.

In the case of a giftmoot economy, it may seem that examples are difficult to find, because a giftmoot economy doesn't currently exist.  It is the job of a familiarity campaign, then, to make visible as many of these examples as possible, and to provide people with a toolkit with which to find them.  I'll describe some of that here, and build on it further in a section on imaginative exercises.

## Of the concept of a giftmoot economy

The first, most basic thing to do is to get people familiar with the existence of the concept at all, and that means some sort of branding and some sort of publicity campaign, through whatever channels are relevant, such as social media, in-person meetings, flyers, traditional advertising or whatever else.  I won't pretend to know the best practices of such a campaign, but the first central aim is for people to have heard of the concept (perhaps through the word "giftmoot") and then to have some basic definition or association with that word.

The next step is to make present some simple and intuitive description that everyone can fall back on.  This can be tricky.  For example, consider the word "socialism".  There is very poor recognition of what socialism might mean across a range of demographics: it could mean "when the government pays for stuff", or "single-party authoritarian government" or "common ownership of the means of production" or "a stateless, classless society".  It could connote violent revolution, or it could suggest progressive reform.  And sometimes two socialists don't even agree on whether the other is a "true" socialist because they could be using different definitions or different measures.

Moreover, "common ownership of the means of production", even if it were the gold-standard that had widespread recognition, is a bit ambiguous to a lot of people.  What are the "means of production"?  How does the common ownership work?  Does it imply equal distribution?

So finding a simple and intuitive way of describing the concept is paramount.  I have tended to say, "an economy that replaces exchanges with gifting, where there is no expectation or obligation to provide anything in return".  Of course, I am mostly talking to myself, so whether this is an intuitive way of describing it for most people is largely untested.


## Where the concept comes from

An intuitive pathway into this concept, and a concrete link to the current system, is to indicate that a giftmoot economy is an extension of some of the most essential, beneficial and under-examined parts of the current system.  That is, gift-giving is already everywhere, it is some of the most essential and moral action in the economy, and it comes in a wide variety of forms - personal, community-oriented, from businesses, from governments.

This framing emphasises that a giftmoot economy is not an idea that comes from nowhere and is completely untested, but rather that it is an idea that is already currently and ubiquitously implemented and is what makes society function.  It almost all cases, problems created by markets are solved through gift-giving.

This adds credibility, but also helps place gift-giving into a clear, practical context.  For example, when I raise gift-giving, a lot of people think of birthday presents, but when it is placed in a context of welfare, charity, volunteering, personal support, unpaid labour and mutual aid, people have a very different, but concrete and clear, idea of what gift-giving involves.

It also makes the entire concept less alien and idealistic, because it is already a widespread and necessary practice, and highlights some of the issues that the market economy poses in an immediate and intuitive way.  Welfare for people with a disability is accepted as a moral practice and one that cannot really be solved in a more efficient manner, for example, and there are many more cases to draw from without having to go far beyond a person's immediate experience.

## Of how the concept works

Next there needs to be familiarity with the way that the concept of a giftmoot economy works.  This is going to be essential for building a toolkit that people can use to identify examples that build up their tangible connection with the idea.  For example, I propose that when there is no exchange-value, we are likely to produce more socially beneficial things for people who can't provide much labour, or that a mansion will have less use-value and more cost-value if labour can choose not to maintain it for the owner.  Each of these takes the premises of a non-reciprocal gifting economy and reasons from them towards outcomes, using the tools to come to conclusions.  However, I don't think that either of those statements is very intuitive or transparent, especially as written.

It might then be worth breaking down the concept into a series of conclusions that are sufficiently broad to be applicable to a variety of examples, but sufficiently narrow that they are easy to learn and remember, such as, "In a giftmoot economy, people who can't work can still receive goods and services" or "In a giftmoot economy, workers can choose only to do work they believe in".

But it may be possible to go one step further as well, and formulate a few main questions that a person could ask in any circumstance:

- "If my basic needs were met, how would I behave differently in this scenario?"
- "Do we solve a problem here by giving people something for free?"
- "If no one could sell this thing, how might they treat it differently?"

These are obviously not questions that completely describe the context of a giftmoot economy, but they can perhaps provide a very intuitive insight into the ways that some things might be different, or how some things are already handled through gifting, and in a manner that a person can carry from context to context and continually reapply, allowing them to explore the situation easily and intuitively.

## Tangible connection

To being someone all the way to familiarity, they need to feel a tangible connection to the idea.  By this I mean it needs to be taken out of the abstract and placed into a more concrete context.  I don't mean that it needs to be practiced, because in many cases that it impossible to do.  But it needs to feel like it could be plausibly practiced, rather than being some vague idealistic idea.

One way to begin this process is to provide answers to the above questions.  Rather than having a familiarity campaign that talks only about abstract concepts, it would be good to have a campaign that can identify lots of specific scenarios that the viewer, reader or listener can imagine themselves in.  So a primary part of the campaign would be to collect plentiful examples of answers to the previous questions and then continue to drip-feed the examples to people so that they have constant, gentle reminders about what a giftmoot economy would be like (or how gifting is already an essential and beneficial part of the current economy).

## How to find out more

More than just being tangible, the concept will feel fleshed out and comprehensive if there is some further place to go to learn more, and where it feels like any questions that arise from the process can be answered.  This is pretty powerful even if the viewer of the campaign doesn't actually go there and read anything or engage - the fact that the campaign does not seem superficial lends it credibility and groundedness.

There therefore needs to be some repository of further knowledge, whether that is a forum, a website, a group, a newsletter, a social media page, this site, or some other place that is easily accessible.
