# Consideration in resource allocation

Hopefully at this point I have made a fair case that people will do jobs, and people will do the right sort of jobs to direct resources towards the things that make a healthy economy: satisfying people's genuine needs without too much waste.  Another part of economic calculation is the ability for businesses and consumers to make rational decisions about what goods to acquire.  This is associated, at least in part, with the idea that in an exchange economy prices will clearly signal which resources are most beneficial to use at the time due to supply and demand considerations.  For example, if a fork manufacturer is looking to make their forks out of one of a variety of useful metals, which one should they obtain?  Well, in an exchange economy one clear signal they can use is price: the cheapest metal is likely in highest supply and/or lowest demand, meaning that it would be socially beneficial to use that metal over the alternatives because there is a sufficient amount available to use that isn't being taken away from some other high-demand production.  Can a non-reciprocal gifting economy perform as well?

Once again, I think the answer is yes.  There are two main factors here that I think are relevant - one set that are relatively straightforward that I will talk about here, and one set that I will talk about in the next section on giftmoots.

In this article I'll talk about the practical and social considerations that we already make, and in the next few articles I'll talk about ways that goods can be allocated without using prices.

## Practical consideration

There are already a number of ways in which in people must make considerations about resource allocation that don't rely on prices - in fact, the vast majority of these precede pricing considerations.  And that set of considerations is simply what a person needs to do what they want to do, and whether what they want to do is practical.
